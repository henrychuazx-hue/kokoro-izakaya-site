TIER 3 — THE ARCADE CLASSICS: THE HONEST ANSWER

This is the section every other storefront lies about, so read it before you buy from anyone — including us.

Time Crisis, Point Blank, Lethal Enforcers, Duck Hunt, Cabal and the 1995 arcade Area 51 have no legal purchase path on PC. None. At any price. They are not on Steam, not in the official arcade compilations, not in any subscription. The only officially licensed way to play Time Crisis or Point Blank at home in 2026 is inside a competitor's sealed mini-console — a different product from ours, and we won't pretend otherwise.

So when a listing promises you those titles on a drive, it isn't offering you a licence nobody else could get. It's offering you copies.

What is legitimately buyable from this era — with the honest caveat that both need community software to work with a light gun, because the publishers shipped them without proper gun support:

And one genre trap worth knowing: classic first-person shooters — the Doom and Wolfenstein lineage — are legally easy to get hold of and mechanically wrong for this hardware. A light gun reports where it's pointing; those games expect relative mouse movement for mouselook. The result is spinning, not aiming. Anyone selling you a light gun on the promise of classic FPS games hasn't tried it.

◆ WHERE WE STAND ON GAME FILES

No game software is included with, bundled with, supplied by, or available from RetroTrigger. We have never shipped game files and we don't intend to. We also won't help you find them — not by link, not by email, not by hint — because that's the line between selling hardware and distributing other people's work, and we'd like to still be here next year.

You must own the games you play. Emulators themselves are lawful software; that was settled in the US courts decades ago (Sony v. Connectix, Sega v. Accolade). Game files are a separate matter: ROMs, disc images and console BIOS files are each independently copyrighted, and copying them without permission is infringement regardless of how old the game is or whether it's still sold.

One correction to something you'll read everywhere: plenty of retro sellers tell you it's fine to rip a disc you legally own. Be careful with that. US law treats breaking a game's copy protection as a separate violation from infringement under the DMCA's anti-circumvention rules, and the current exemptions cover libraries, archives and museums — not consumers backing up their own shelves. Ownership of the disc does not by itself make the circumvention lawful. We're telling you this because a seller who pretends otherwise is transferring their risk onto you.

Not legal advice. We're a hardware company, not lawyers, copyright law varies by country, and it's your call in your jurisdiction.
